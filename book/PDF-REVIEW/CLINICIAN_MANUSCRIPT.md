# CHAPTER 4 — SAFETY SCREENING

# 4 · Before You Start

This is the chapter to read first, and it is deliberately placed before any instruction.

A book cannot examine you. I cannot feel where your pain is, ask what makes it worse, notice that your gait is asymmetric, or take a history. Everything in the following chapter is written for a general reader with a common, non-specific musculoskeletal complaint, and it is entirely possible that you are not that reader. This chapter is how you find out.

## The things that need a clinician, not a ball

Start with the short list. These are reasons to seek assessment rather than to begin self-treatment, and some are reasons to seek it today.

**Go to an emergency department or call emergency services the same day if you have:**

- Numbness in the saddle area — between the legs, around the genitals or anus
- New difficulty passing urine, inability to pass urine, incontinence, or loss of bowel control
- Weakness or numbness in both legs
- A calf that is swollen, hot, red, and tender, particularly if it came on suddenly
- Sudden severe neck pain or a headache unlike any you have had, especially with neck movement
- New dizziness, double vision, slurred speech, facial numbness, or difficulty swallowing, particularly after moving your neck or after a neck injury
- Chest pain, breathlessness, or pain brought on by exertion
- A fever with severe back pain, or unexplained fever alongside new spinal pain

The first three on that list together describe the classic presentation of cauda equina syndrome, a compression of the nerve roots below the spinal cord. A 2025 review of international guidelines found that perineal sensory change and bladder or bowel dysfunction appear in all of the guideline documents examined, with radicular pain in most and new motor weakness in about half. All agree that suspected cauda equina syndrome requires urgent imaging. It is rare. It is also one of the few situations in musculoskeletal care where a delay of days rather than hours can change the outcome permanently, which is why it is at the top of a book about tennis balls.

**See a clinician within days, not weeks, if you have:**

- Pain that has worsened steadily over two to three weeks despite sensible activity
- Pain that consistently wakes you at night
- Unexplained weight loss
- New back or bone pain with a history of cancer
- Pain after a significant fall, collision, or accident
- Progressive stiffness accompanied by systemic symptoms such as fever or malaise
- Any symptom that frightens you, even if you cannot say why

## Why this list is not a warning about you

There is a failure mode in writing about red flags, and it runs in two directions.

The first is to present the list as a threat, which produces a reader who is now afraid of their own back and less able to move, which is the opposite of useful. The second is to attach a reassuring statistic so that the list can be waved away, which is how a delayed diagnosis happens.

Neither is honest, so here is the accurate version. In one large study of 1,172 people who consulted a family doctor with acute low back pain, 11 — just under one per cent — turned out to have a serious spinal condition, mostly vertebral fractures. But 80 per cent of that whole group had at least one warning sign, and the doctors missed more than half of the serious cases at the first visit.

Two things follow from those numbers, and they point in opposite directions. Warning signs are common and they are not a diagnosis; having one does not mean something is seriously wrong. And warning signs are not a substitute for judgement, which is why the instruction is to get checked rather than to assess yourself. That study concerned acute low back pain in primary care. It tells you nothing about neck pain, nothing about long-standing pain, and nothing about anyone already under specialist care.

## Conditions that change the rules

The following are not necessarily reasons to avoid all self-treatment, but they change what is safe, and several are absolute exclusions for particular techniques.

| Condition | What changes |
|---|---|
| **Pregnancy** | Avoid lying on your back after the first trimester; avoid abdominal pressure; ask your midwife or clinician before starting |
| **Anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication; bleeding disorders** | Bruising risk is higher; use substantially less pressure, and get advice first |
| **Reduced sensation** — peripheral neuropathy, diabetes with neuropathy, spinal cord injury | **Do not use pressure you cannot feel accurately.** The feedback loop that makes this safe is absent |
| **Severe osteoporosis** | Avoid loading over bone; no pressure on the spine |
| **Recent surgery** | No work on or near the site until cleared by the surgeon |
| **Known or suspected deep vein thrombosis** | **Absolute exclusion in the affected limb** |
| **Peripheral arterial disease** | Avoid calf pressure |
| **Varicose veins** | Avoid direct pressure on the veins |
| **Active cancer** | Get advice before any technique |
| **Hypermobility spectrum disorder or hEDS** | Reduce end-range work; prioritise control over range |
| **Acute injury, bruising, swelling, open wound, or skin infection** | Avoid the area entirely |
| **Fever or acute illness** | Defer until you are well |

The international expert consensus on contraindications to myofascial work is unusually clear on a few of these. Local tissue inflammation drew 97 per cent agreement as a contraindication, deep vein thrombosis 97 per cent, bone infection 94 per cent, myositis ossificans 92 per cent, fracture 84 per cent, and open wounds 73 per cent. Those figures are the closest thing this field has to a settled position on safety.

## The rule that governs everything else

If you take one instruction from this chapter, take this one.

**When in doubt, don't.**

The asymmetry is stark. If you skip a session you were unsure about, you have lost fifteen minutes. If you proceed with one you should have skipped, you may lose considerably more. A book cannot examine you, which means the judgement has to sit with you, and the correct default when that judgement is uncertain is not to act.

There is a second rule, equally short, that will appear again in the routine: **pain is a stop signal, not a target.** The reasons are in the next chapter, but the practice is simple. Sharp pain, pain that travels down a limb, numbness, tingling, weakness, dizziness, or a burning sensation all mean stop. Soreness afterwards that lasts a few hours is common; a flare that carries into the next day means less pressure and less time, not more.

## What this chapter can and cannot do

It can tell you when to stop and when to seek help. It can tell you which conditions make specific techniques unsafe. It can give you a list that a careful clinician would want you to know.

It cannot examine you, and it cannot diagnose you. If you finish this chapter unsure whether the routine is appropriate for you, that uncertainty is the answer: ask someone qualified before you begin. A physiotherapist, a GP, or a specialist musculoskeletal clinician can answer in ten minutes a question this book can only approach in fifteen pages.

One last thing, which matters more than it may seem. Nothing in this chapter, and nothing anywhere in this book, implies that your pain is not real, that it is your fault, or that you should have to manage it alone. Self-treatment is one small option among several. For some readers it will be a useful part of a larger plan. For others the right answer is professional care, and for some it is care plus time plus nothing else for a while. All of those are legitimate outcomes, and none of them is a failure.

# CHAPTER 5 — TECHNIQUES

# 5 · The Three Tools, and the Routine

Before any technique, three principles. They are the whole of the method, and everything in this chapter is an application of them.

**Work at three or four out of ten.** Not five, not seven. Find it the first time by starting at almost nothing and increasing slowly until you can feel it clearly and could comfortably fall asleep. That is three.

**Short holds, repeated.** Twenty to sixty seconds, a few times, with breathing. One to three minutes per area in total. There is no validated dose for any of this; those numbers are a practical starting point, not a finding from a trial, and I would rather say so than dress them up.

**Stop on any signal.** Sharp pain, pain travelling down an arm or leg, numbness, tingling, weakness, dizziness, visual change, or a burning sensation. And the next-day rule: soreness for a few hours is normal, a flare that lasts into the next day means less pressure and less time.

## Why gentler is not the timid option

The instinct to press harder when something isn't working is nearly universal, and it is worth understanding why it fails.

Pain modulates pain. Pressing hard enough to hurt produces a nociceptive input of its own, and the nervous system responds by increasing protective activity in the area — the guarding you were trying to reduce. You have not opened a window; you have prompted the tissue to defend itself. Meanwhile the sensation is intense enough to occupy your attention, which many people read as effectiveness. It is not. Intensity is a measure of how much stimulation you applied, not of how much useful change occurred.

A harder ball concentrates force. A tennis ball is about 58 millimetres across and deforms under load, which spreads the force over a wider area and limits how much pressure any one point can receive. That compliance is a safety feature, not a compromise. A lacrosse ball, a golf ball, or anything with knobs concentrates force into a small area over structures you cannot see, and it does so for no demonstrated additional benefit. I am not going to recommend them.

## The three tools

**The tennis ball** is the workhorse. Small enough to be specific, soft enough to be self-limiting, cheap enough to own several.

**The wall** is the most underrated part of the kit. Working against a wall lets you control the amount of force with your legs rather than with your arms, which means you can back off instantly by straightening a knee. It also makes the lower back and shoulders accessible without lying on the floor with your full body weight on a ball.

**The towel** does two jobs. Rolled, it changes the angle of a stretch or supports a limb so that you can relax into it. Folded, it can provide gentle traction that a ball cannot.

What you do not need: a foam roller is useful for broad areas and is not dangerous, but it is large and difficult to control near the spine, and it is not necessary for anything in this book. A massage ball is a reasonable substitute for the tennis ball if it is similarly compliant.

## The routine

Fifteen minutes, three blocks. Two minutes to settle, nine minutes of body work, four minutes of movement. The movement block is not optional and is the part that matters most; I will explain why at the end.

Do two or three regions per session, not all of them. The weekly template below rotates so that no two consecutive days repeat the same pair.

| Day | Regions | Movement |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Neck-adjacent and upper back | Neck rotation, scapular setting |
| Tuesday | Hip and gluteal | Hip hinge, hip rotation |
| Wednesday | Upper back and shoulders | Thoracic extension, scapular setting |
| Thursday | Hip and thigh | Hip rotation, walking |
| Friday | Neck-adjacent and upper back | Neck rotation, scapular setting |
| Saturday | Hip and gluteal | Hip hinge, walking |
| Sunday | Nothing, or five minutes | Walk |

## Working around the neck, not on it

This is the section where the book's caution is least negotiable, so I will put the rule before the technique.

**Do not manipulate your own neck.** No thrusting, no forcing it to end range, no trying to make it crack. A 2026 systematic review of adverse events following cervical self-manipulation identified 24 reported cases. Seventy-one per cent were male, the mean age was 34, and half had neck pain or stiffness beforehand — which is to say, these were people doing what you might be tempted to do. Twelve of the 24 were severe or catastrophic, and two were fatal. Twenty-two of the 24 occurred within two days. Fifteen involved the person's own hands. Most of the events were vascular.

Those are case reports, the weakest form of evidence, and the true incidence is genuinely unknown — a systematic review of the association between cervical manipulation and carotid dissection found no epidemiological study that measured it. So I am not telling you this will happen to you. I am telling you that the manoeuvre has no benefit that would justify even a small risk, which is why this book does not teach it.

What you do instead is work the tissue *around* the neck. Upper trapezius with the ball against the wall, at the shoulder rather than at the neck. The upper medial border of the scapula, for the muscles that attach there. The thoracic spine with a rolled towel, because a great deal of what people experience as neck stiffness originates in the upper back, which is a different structure with a different risk profile.

Avoid entirely: the front and sides of the neck, where the carotid arteries and airway are; the back of the neck directly over the cervical spine; the base of the skull with pressure; and anything that reproduces dizziness, visual change, or nausea.

## Hips and glutes

The gluteal region tolerates more pressure than the neck, and responds well to a ball used seated or against a wall. Place the ball under one buttock, lean back, and control the load by how much weight you take through that side. Work the outer and upper part.

Two cautions. The sciatic nerve runs deep through this region; sustained deep pressure there is not appropriate, and any reproduction of leg pain, tingling, or numbness is a stop signal rather than a target to work through. And the iliotibial band — the thick band down the outside of the thigh — cannot be stretched or broken down by pressure, and heavy direct work on it is uncomfortable and mechanically pointless. Work the muscle around it instead: gluteus medius at the hip, vastus lateralis on the outer thigh.

Hip flexors respond better to a towel than a ball. Lie on your side with a rolled towel under the hip, or use a supported half-kneeling position and let the hip settle rather than forcing it.

## Lower back, thighs, feet

**The lower back is where self-treatment most often becomes self-injury.** The rule is no direct pressure on the spine or over the spinous processes, and no lying on a ball on the floor with your full body weight. Where you work is the paraspinal muscle either side of the spine, with the ball against a wall so that your legs control the load. There is also a mechanical reason for the caution that is worth knowing: the thoracolumbar fascia is thick and largely not accessible to a ball in any case, so the dramatic version of this technique was never doing what it claimed.

**Calf work carries one absolute rule.** If you have any risk factor for deep vein thrombosis — recent surgery, prolonged immobility, long-haul travel, a clotting disorder, active cancer, pregnancy, or oestrogen therapy — do not apply pressure to the calf. A calf that is unilaterally swollen, warm, red, and tender needs urgent assessment, not massage.

**Thighs** are forgiving. Quadriceps and hamstrings with a ball or roller, broad and moderate, avoiding the outer knee and the head of the fibula where the common peroneal nerve runs.

**Feet** are the safest and most reliably pleasant region in the book. Seated, ball under the arch, control the weight through your leg. Nothing bad happens here, which makes it a reasonable place to start if you are uncertain about the rest.

## The five-minute version

There will be weeks when fifteen minutes is not available. The reduced version is one region and one movement, five minutes, and it is not a failure state. It is the maintenance dose, and it is better than nothing precisely because it survives a bad week.

## The part that matters

Here is the honest accounting. If you only had time for one thing, it would not be the ball. The strongest evidence in this book concerns exercise: a Cochrane review found moderate-certainty evidence that exercise reduces chronic low back pain compared with no treatment, and no difference when exercise was compared directly with manual therapy. For neck pain, the evidence favours strengthening rather than stretching, and the guidance from NICE on low back pain considers manual therapy including soft-tissue work only as part of a treatment package that includes exercise.

That last phrase describes the ball's real role exactly. It is the part that makes the area comfortable enough to move, and the movement is what changes the trajectory. Which means the four minutes at the end of the routine are not a warm-down. They are the point, and the nine minutes before them are what allow you to do them.

Four movements, none of them requiring equipment: neck rotation within comfort, a scapular setting exercise, a hip hinge, and walking. Keep them unremarkable. Nothing in this book requires you to be athletic, and the best movement is the one you will still be doing in six weeks.

# CHAPTER 7 — QUICK REFERENCE (STOP RULES)

# 7 · Quick Reference

The book is short by design, and this chapter is the part you will use. Everything below is compressed from the chapters before it, and nothing here is new.

## The rules

Work at three or four out of ten. Short holds of twenty to sixty seconds, a few times. One to three minutes per area. Stop on any sharp pain, radiating pain, numbness, tingling, weakness, dizziness, or burning. Soreness for a few hours is normal; a flare into the next day means less pressure and less time. When in doubt, don't.

## Stop now, and get emergency help, if you have

Saddle numbness. New bladder or bowel problems, including difficulty passing urine or loss of control. Weakness or numbness in both legs. A calf that is swollen, hot, red, and tender. Sudden severe neck pain or a new severe headache, particularly with neck movement. New dizziness, double vision, slurred speech, facial numbness, or difficulty swallowing. Chest pain or breathlessness. Fever with severe back pain.

## See a clinician within days if you have

Pain worsening steadily over two to three weeks. Night pain that wakes you. Unexplained weight loss. New spinal pain with a history of cancer. Pain after a significant fall or collision. Stiffness with systemic symptoms. Any symptom that frightens you.

## Do not apply pressure if you have

Reduced sensation you cannot feel accurately. Known or suspected deep vein thrombosis, or calf risk factors for one. Skin infection, open wound, acute bruising, or swelling in the area. A recent fracture or surgery in the area. Severe osteoporosis, over bone or spine. Active cancer, without advice first. Fever or acute illness.

Use substantially less pressure, and ask first, if you take anticoagulants or antiplatelets, are pregnant, or have a bleeding disorder or hypermobility spectrum condition.

## Where not to press

Front and sides of the neck. Directly over the cervical spine. The base of the skull with pressure. Over the spinous processes anywhere. The axilla. Directly over the sciatic path in the deep gluteal region. The head of the fibula. Directly on varicose veins. The calf, if any thrombosis risk factor is present.

## The fifteen minutes

Two minutes settling and breathing. Nine minutes of body work, two or three regions, at three or four out of ten. Four minutes of movement, which is the part that matters. Five-minute version on bad weeks: one region, one movement.

## The weekly template

Neck-adjacent and upper back on Monday, Wednesday, Friday. Hip and gluteal on Tuesday and Saturday. Hip and thigh on Thursday. Sunday off or a walk. This is one arrangement, not a prescription; what matters is that you rotate rather than repeat, and that you do not attempt everything in one session.

## The four movements

Neck rotation, slowly, within comfort. Scapular setting — draw the shoulder blades back and down without shrugging. Hip hinge — push the hips back with a soft knee and a long spine. Walking, to a distance you would do on an ordinary day.

## Two weeks of noticing

One repeatable measure, taken the same way at the same time. One 0–10 rating once a day. What you did. Whether the next day was worse. Read the direction over a fortnight, ignore single bad days, and decide at the end: continue, reduce, or stop and get advice.

## If nothing changes

Four to six weeks of consistent practice with no movement in your recorded measure and no change in how much the problem interferes with your life is a clear answer. Stop, and spend the time on something else. That is a good outcome, arrived at honestly, and it is better than another year of a practice that is not working.

## What this book was, and what it was not

It was an attempt to describe one small, cheap, low-risk practice accurately, and to be clear about the limits of what it can do.

It was not a cure, and it does not contain one. It did not claim that pressure restructures your fascia, breaks up adhesions, or releases anything that could be named; chapter three explains why those claims do not hold. It did not tell you that your pain is imaginary if your scan is normal, and it did not tell you that your scan explains your pain. It did not suggest that self-treatment replaces assessment, and it did not offer a diagnosis of any kind.

Three things are worth carrying out of it.

The first is that pain is real and produced rather than simply received, which means your experience is not in question even when its cause is uncertain. The second is that the strongest evidence in this territory concerns movement, not pressure, and the ball's honest job is to make movement tolerable. The third is that the judgement about when to stop belongs to you, and that exercising it is not a failure of the practice but part of it.

If the book has done its work, the morning check has changed slightly. It is still there — I still do mine — but it is no longer a verdict delivered before you are fully awake. It is a report from a system you understand a little better than you did, followed by a decision you are equipped to make.

That is a modest thing. It is also, as far as the evidence goes, the true one.

## Glossary

**Cauda equina syndrome.** Compression of the bundle of nerve roots below the spinal cord, producing saddle numbness and bladder or bowel dysfunction. A surgical emergency.

**Certainty (of evidence).** A graded judgement, in systematic reviews, of how confident we can be that an estimated effect is close to the true one. "Moderate" is a specific rating, not a synonym for "proven."

**Contraindication.** A condition in which a technique should not be used, either absolutely or with modification.

**Densification.** A proposed change in which fascial layers become more adhesive and glide less freely. Supported by rheological, imaging, and therapeutic evidence; not confirmed histologically in humans.

**Fascia.** Connective tissue forming sheets and sheaths beneath the skin, enclosing and separating muscle and organs. See Chapter 2 for the two competing formal definitions.

**Fascial system.** The broader of the two definitions: the three-dimensional continuum of collagen-containing connective tissue throughout the body.

**Modic changes.** Changes in the vertebral bone adjacent to a disc, visible on MRI, of uncertain clinical significance.

**Myofascial release.** A family of manual techniques, practitioner-applied or self-applied, involving sustained pressure on soft tissue. Evidence for short-term benefit on pain is moderate in chronic low back pain and limited by lack of blinding in neck pain.

**Nociception.** The nervous system's detection and encoding of potentially damaging stimuli. Distinct from pain, which is the experience.

**Nociplastic pain.** Pain arising from altered nociception without clear evidence of tissue damage or nerve lesion. A mechanism descriptor, not a diagnosis.

**Red flag.** A symptom or history feature associated with a serious underlying condition, which warrants assessment rather than self-treatment.

**Spondylolysis.** A defect in the bony arch of a vertebra.
