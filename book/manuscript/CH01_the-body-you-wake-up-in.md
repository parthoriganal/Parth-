# Chapter 1
## The Body You Wake Up In

Before you get out of bed, you check.

Most people with a stiff neck or an aching hip do this without deciding to. You turn your head a little against the pillow and wait for the answer. You roll one shoulder back and see how far it goes. You notice, in the first conscious second of the day, whether today is a good day or one of the other kind. Then you get up and arrange the morning around what you found.

It is a strange habit when you look at it directly. You are consulting your body about the day before the day has happened. But it makes sense, because the answer changes things. It changes what you reach for, how you sit, whether you take the stairs, whether you say yes to something in the afternoon. A stiff neck at seven in the morning is not just a sensation. It is a forecast, and you plan around it.

This book is about that moment. Not about erasing it. I want to be careful with you from the first page, because a great deal of what is written about fascia and self-treatment is not careful. It is about changing what the moment means. At the moment, for most people, the morning check is a verdict. Something is wrong, and you would like to know how wrong. By the end of this book I would like it to be information. Here is how today is, here is what I can do about it in the next fifteen minutes, and here is when I should stop doing anything and ask someone.

That is a smaller promise than the ones you have probably been offered. It is also the only one I can keep.

### What pain is, and what it is not

There is a definition that most of the medical world uses, and it is worth knowing because it is more careful than the way we usually talk.

The International Association for the Study of Pain, whose definitions are the ones most clinicians work from, describes pain as an unpleasant experience that combines sensation and emotion. On their account it arises in connection with tissue that is actually damaged or potentially at risk of damage, or with something that closely resembles it.

Notice where the weight falls in that sentence. *Experience.* Not damage. Not a signal with a fixed volume. The definition puts pain among the things that happen to a person. Fear, hunger and grief are nearby. A bruise or a fracture is not. The word *unpleasant* is doing real work too: pain is not a neutral measurement that happens to be uncomfortable. The unpleasantness is part of what it is.

The association that published that definition adds a second point in its notes, and this is the one that matters most for the chapters ahead. Pain and nociception are different things. Nociception is the nervous system's process of detecting and encoding potentially damaging stimuli — a physical, measurable event. Pain is what a person experiences. You cannot work out how much pain someone is in by measuring the activity in their sensory nerves, because the relationship between the two is not fixed.

A plain example. You are absorbed in something and you burn your hand on a pan. For a second or two there is no pain at all — just the sight of it, and the movement of pulling away. Then it arrives. The tissue was damaged at the moment of contact. The pain came later, and it came from your nervous system's interpretation of the event, not from the event itself. Everyone has had some version of this. The opposite happens too: a wound that should hurt, that everyone agrees should hurt, and that for some reason does not very much.

None of this means pain is imaginary. It means pain is produced. Your nervous system constructs it, using information about tissue state, but also using attention, expectation, memory, fear, fatigue, and how safe the situation feels. That is not a flaw in the way the system works. It is how the system has always worked, and it is why pain can be both entirely real and a poor measure of how much damage is present.

The association makes one further point in the notes attached to that definition, and it is the rule this book is written under. When a person says they are in pain, what they say should be respected. Not tolerated. Not taken with a grain of salt. Respected.

The reason is simple. You are the only person with direct access to what you feel. Nothing in the pages that follow is going to suggest that you are mistaken about it.

### The scan problem

If you have had an aching hip or a stiff neck for more than a few months, the chances are good that someone has imaged it. And if they did, the chances are good that the report contained a finding — some word like *degeneration*, *bulge*, *tear*, *loss of height* — that sounded like an explanation.

It is worth knowing what those words mean in a body that has no pain at all.

In 2015, a group of researchers pooled 33 studies covering 3,110 people who had no back pain whatsoever — volunteers, not patients — and looked at how common these findings were in their spines. The results are startling if you have never seen them, and old news if you work in the field. Disc degeneration was present in 37 per cent of people in their twenties, and in 96 per cent of people in their eighties. A disc bulge, the finding that frightens people most, was present in 30 per cent of twenty-year-olds and 84 per cent of eighty-year-olds.

None of these people had back pain. They were simply people whose spines looked like that, in the way that most of us will eventually have knees and hips that look like something. And to be clear about the other reading: a finding this common is not a sentence. That does not make the finding invented, and it does not mean it will never matter. It means that on its own it cannot tell you what your back will do next year.

The authors were careful about what their data did and did not show. Their conclusion was that many of these findings probably belong to ordinary ageing rather than to disease, and on their own need not mean pain. They added a caveat I want to keep attached to that: an imaging finding still has to be read alongside the person in front of the clinician, not instead of them.

If you have been carrying a scan result around as though it were a diagnosis, this may be the single most useful thing in the book, and I want you to have it early.

But I am not going to leave it there, because the version of this argument that gets passed around online is not honest. The same research group published a companion review in the same year, looking at whether these findings are *more* common in people who do have back pain. Some of them are, and the pattern is more interesting than a simple yes.

They pooled fourteen studies and 3,097 people aged fifty or under, about four in ten of them without back pain. In that group, disc bulges were substantially more common in people with pain, and so were disc degeneration, disc extrusion and protrusion, Modic 1 changes, and spondylolysis. But other findings showed no clear association with pain at all — Modic changes taken as a whole, central canal stenosis, and the high-intensity zone that shows up in so many reports.

So the honest position is not "scans are meaningless." It is narrower, and stranger: some structural findings track with pain in younger adults and some do not, and which is which is not something you can read off your own report.

**Imaging tells you what your spine looks like. It does not tell you what you will feel tomorrow.**

Both halves of that sentence have to be held at once. The finding on your scan is real. It is also extremely common in people who feel fine, and it is a poor predictor of what any given person will experience. A scan is a photograph of structure, and pain is not a structure. It is an event that happens to a person, in a body, in a life.

Which brings me to the sentence I most want to prevent you from hearing. It is the one people take away from this material, and it does real harm: *if your scan is normal, your pain isn't real.* No. A normal scan means the photograph did not show the thing the photograph is good at showing. It does not mean the pain is not happening, and it certainly does not mean there is nothing to do. It means the answer is somewhere the camera cannot see.

### What this chapter is not saying

There is a temptation, on learning that pain and tissue damage are not the same thing, to run with it. To decide that the body doesn't matter, that it's all nervous system, that you could think your way out of this. The evidence doesn't support that, and neither does anything in this book.

Tissue state is one input into pain, and it is the one most responsive to what you do: load, position, how you spend your hours. It is not the only input, and it is not a dial you can turn directly.

What follows is therefore less comfortable than either simple story. Your pain is real. Your tissue is real. The relationship between them is looser than "something is damaged" or "it's all in your head" would suggest, and the rest of the book works inside that looseness rather than trying to resolve it.

### If this is the first book like this you have read

You may have picked this up expecting to be told that your fascia is stuck, that it needs releasing, and that the tennis ball on the cover is the tool for the job. Some of that is true and some of it is not, and the parts that are not are going to be said plainly in Chapter 3, with the evidence attached.

What I can promise is narrower and, I think, better. By the end of the book you will know what fascia is and where the science on it is genuinely unsettled. You will know exactly what a ball can and cannot do. You will have a specific routine that takes about fifteen minutes and uses three things you already own, with clear instructions and clearer reasons to stop. And you will know when self-treatment is the wrong answer — which is not a failure state, and which is the chapter I would most like you to read even if you read nothing else.

The morning check is not going to disappear. I still do it. But it can stop being a verdict and start being a report from a system you understand a little better than you did, which is a modest thing that changes how a day goes.

> ### What we know, and what we don't
>
> **We know** that pain is an experience produced by the nervous system, not a direct readout of tissue damage, and that this is the mainstream scientific position rather than a fringe one.
>
> **We know** that common spinal imaging findings appear in most people with no pain at all, that some of them are nonetheless more common in younger adults who do have pain, and that others are not.
>
> **We know** that a person's account of their own pain should be respected, and that the association says so in the notes that accompany its definition.
>
> **We don't know**, for any one finding on any one scan, whether it is contributing to that person's pain. Two people with identical scans and identical habits can have entirely different experiences, and we cannot currently predict which will be which.
>
> **We don't know** how to reverse the relationship between tissue state and pain once it has become unhelpful. What we have are ways of influencing it — movement, load, sleep, reassurance, time — none of which is a switch.

---

### The short version

Pain is real and it is produced, not simply received. Your scan describes your structure, and the structure does not dictate the experience. Neither half of that lets anyone tell you that what you feel isn't happening — including me.
