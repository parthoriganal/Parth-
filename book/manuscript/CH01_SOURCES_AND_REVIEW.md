## 3. THE BRINJIKJI CONCLUSION — WHAT WAS ACTUALLY WRONG

This default was worth applying, because my earlier draft was worse than I had credited.

**What I had written:** *"The authors drew the conclusion the data supports: many of these imaging features are probably part of normal ageing, and are not associated with pain. Not 'are not always associated with pain.' Not 'should be interpreted with caution.' Part of normal ageing, unassociated with pain, in the majority of people who have them."*

**Two problems, and the second is serious:**

1. **It tracked the source's wording closely** — "part of normal aging and unassociated with pain" is nearly verbatim from the abstract. Attributed, and short, but closer than it needed to be.

2. **It dropped the authors' own limitation.** The paper's conclusion does not stop at "unassociated with pain." It continues: *these imaging findings must be interpreted in the context of the patient's clinical condition.* My draft turned a careful two-part conclusion into a confident one-part claim, and then **emphasised** the unqualified half with rhetorical repetition ("Not… Not… Part of normal ageing, unassociated with pain"). I was amplifying the part of the source that supported my argument and deleting the part that qualified it.

That is the same failure mode as the asymptomatic-cohort inference: a real finding, overstated in the direction I found convenient. **The corrected text now carries both halves, in the authors' own proportion:**

> The authors were careful about what their data did and did not show. Their conclusion was that many of these findings probably belong to ordinary ageing rather than to disease, and on their own need not mean pain. They added a caveat I want to keep attached to that: an imaging finding still has to be read alongside the person in front of the clinician, not instead of them.

**Meaning preserved:** "many" (not all), "probably" (not certainly), "ordinary ageing rather than disease", "on their own need not mean pain" (not "never means pain"). **Limitation preserved:** the clinical-context requirement.

## 4. "NOT A SENTENCE" — KEPT, AND PROPERLY BRACKETED

**What I had:** *"…a finding this common is not a sentence. Most of the people in those studies went about their lives without giving their spines a thought."*

**The problem:** the second sentence is an invention. I do not know what those 3,110 volunteers thought about their spines. It is a plausible inference, and it is exactly the kind of warm, human-sounding detail that reads as fact and is not. **Removed.**

**What replaced it** — a guard on both sides, because "not a sentence" can be misread in two opposite ways:

> And to be clear about the other reading: a finding this common is not a sentence. That does not make the finding invented, and it does not mean it will never matter. It means that on its own it cannot tell you what your back will do next year.

- *Against dismissal:* "does not make the finding invented… does not mean it will never matter."
- *Against false reassurance:* "on its own it cannot tell you what your back will do next year" — a statement about predictive limits, not about prognosis.

Note also that the surrounding text no longer lets the finding be waved away. Two paragraphs later the companion study establishes that **some of these findings genuinely are more common in people with pain**, and the chapter names which ones and which ones aren't. The reader cannot finish this chapter thinking imaging is worthless.

## 7. MY HONEST ASSESSMENT OF THE CHAPTER

**What is strong.** The opening is the best writing in it. The scan section still has the genuine turn that makes the chapter worth reading — give the reader the startling number, let the relief land, then refuse to let the relief harden into a new false belief. And the two sentences that do the most protective work are both short: *"A bruise or a fracture is not."* and *"No."*

**What is weakest.** The burn example is the least original paragraph in the book and I know it. It survives because clarity beats novelty in an explanatory book, and because replacing it would mean inventing an anecdote, which I will not do. If you want it gone, it goes.

**The section I would cut first under layout pressure** is "What this chapter is not saying" — 132 words, and it is closer to the line than it was.

**What I remain uneasy about:** the chapter is now quite short-sentenced. Mean 16.3 words, median 14. That is well inside readable nonfiction range, and it suits the voice — but there is a risk it reads as *clipped* to a reader who prefers longer, more discursively confident prose. I have optimised for the reader who is anxious and skimming, which I think is the right reader to optimise for on page one. **If, reading it, you find the rhythm too even, tell me and I will restore two or three longer sentences deliberately.** The variety matters more than the ceiling.
