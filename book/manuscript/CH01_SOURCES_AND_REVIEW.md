## 2. EDITORIAL SELF-REVIEW

An honest assessment. Things I think are working, things I think are weak, and things I got wrong on the way here.

### 2.1 What I think works

**The opening.** It does what the blueprint asked: starts in a scene, in the reader's own experience, with no statistic and no definition on page one. "Before you get out of bed, you check" is six words and most of the target reader will recognise themselves in it. The chapter then earns the right to define pain by first describing the behaviour that the definition explains.

**The scan section is the chapter's engine.** It has a genuine turn in it — the reader is given a startling fact (96% of eighty-year-olds), allowed to feel the relief of it, and then given the counterweight that stops the relief from becoming a new false belief. That structure is honest and it is also more interesting than either half alone.

**The explicit refusal on page 4.** "If your scan is normal, your pain isn't real. No." Stating the harmful inference out loud, in the reader's own words, and then refusing it, is the single most important thing this chapter does. It is also the thing most books in this category get wrong by omission — they make the argument and leave the reader to draw the wrong conclusion.

**The "what this chapter is not saying" section.** Without it, Chapter 1 would licence a reading ("it's all nervous system") that the rest of the book doesn't support. That section costs 200 words and prevents a misreading that would poison Chapters 3 and 13.

### 2.2 What I think is weak

**The burn example is slightly too neat.** "You burn your hand and the pain arrives a second later" is accurate and widely used, but it has been used in every popular pain-science book for fifteen years, and a well-read reader will recognise it as furniture. I kept it because it is the clearest possible illustration and clarity beats novelty in an explanatory book — but it is the least original paragraph in the chapter, and if you want it replaced I would not argue.

**"The body matters. Load matters. Sleep matters."** — the triple repetition is a rhetorical tic I use to signal weight. It appears three times in the chapter in different forms and by the third time it is doing less work than it costs.

**The final section ("If this is the first book like this you have read") is doing too many jobs.** It promises the book's structure, previews Chapter 3, and sets up Chapter 15, all in 180 words. It reads slightly like a table of contents in prose. I think it earns its place because a reader who picked the book up for techniques needs to be told early that the techniques are not the main event — but it is the section I would cut first if the chapter runs long in layout.

**No illustration.** The blueprint specified two figures for this chapter (nociception ≠ pain; imaging findings by age). Producing them at Stage 4 is correct — but reading the chapter as plain text, the imaging section is doing arithmetic in the reader's head ("37 per cent… 96 per cent") that a simple bar chart would do instantly. **I now think Fig 1.2 is the highest-value illustration in the book**, and I would move it earlier in the Stage 4 queue than the blueprint had it.

### 2.3 What I got wrong, and corrected

I want these on the record rather than quietly fixed.

1. **I wrote that the companion Brinjikji paper found degenerative changes "more often in people with pain than in people without it," full stop.** The paper is more specific and more interesting than that: it found *some* findings associated with pain and *others not* — Modic changes overall, central canal stenosis, and high-intensity zone showed no association. My first draft would have overstated a favourable result in a way that happened to support my argument. Corrected, and the corrected version is a better paragraph.

2. **I wrote "that is not a flaw in the system" when I meant "not a flaw in the way the system works."** A small thing, but the first version implies the system could have been designed otherwise, which is a sloppiness that invites a "yes it is, evolution is messy" objection and distracts from the point.

3. **I initially had annular fissures described as "following the same curve" as degeneration and bulges.** The source gives 19% → 29%, which is a much shallower gradient than 37→96 or 30→84. "The same curve" is loose. **Now flagged in §4.3 as an open question** rather than papered over.

## 3. WHY THE CHAPTER IS SHAPED THIS WAY

Three structural decisions worth explaining, since you may want them changed.

**The definition arrives on the third page, not the first.** A reader in pain does not open a book wanting a definition; they open it wanting to be recognised. The first two pages are recognition. By the time the definition lands, the reader has a reason to care what it says.

**The chapter contains no exercises and no body contact.** This is deliberate. Part I is about earning trust, and the fastest way to lose a sceptical reader is to tell them to press on themselves before you have established that you know what you are talking about. The first technique does not appear until Chapter 7.

**The chapter is 2,122 words against a 2,100 target.** I am leaving it there rather than trimming to hit the number. The two words are not the point; if the chapter had wanted 2,400 it would have taken them.

## 4. UNRESOLVED FACTUAL QUESTIONS

Items I could not settle from the sources available to me, listed rather than guessed. **None of these blocks Stage 4** — they are corrections to make before the manuscript is final.

### 4.1 IASP copyright — the one item that needs your decision

**Resolved:** the definition is IASP copyright and their site requires a permissions request for reprinting. **The chapter therefore paraphrases and attributes, and quotes only the 12-word note about respecting a person's report.**

**Unresolved:** whether even that 12-word quotation, in a commercially sold book, is within fair use or needs permission. My view is that it is — it is a short, attributed, transformative use — but **I am not a lawyer and I am not going to assert it.** Options:
- (a) paraphrase that line too ("the association states that a person's account of their own pain should be respected") — costs nothing, removes the question entirely
- (b) request written permission from IASP before publication
- (c) publish as drafted

**My recommendation: (a).** It is free, it is safe, and the sentence is unaffected. I have left it quoted so you can see the alternatives, but I would change it in the next pass.

### 4.2 The annular fissure figures

The source abstract gives fissures rising from 19% to 29%. My sentence says they "followed the same curve," which implies a steep gradient they do not have. Two fixes: cut the sentence entirely (the degeneration and bulge figures make the point), or rewrite it accurately as a shallow rise. **Recommendation: cut it.** Three numbers do not make the argument better than two, and the third is the one I would have to explain.

### 4.3 "Pain is produced, not transmitted" — the closing line

This is a rhetorical compression, and it is *nearly* true but not quite. Nociception genuinely is transmitted, and the chapter says so three pages earlier, which makes the closing line risk reading as a contradiction. It lands well; it is also the kind of sentence a careful clinician would circle. **Flagging for your call.** The safer version is "Pain is real and it is produced, not simply received."

### 4.4 The 38.6% / 61.4% split

I describe the 3,097 people as "about a third of them pain-free." The actual figure is 1,193 asymptomatic (38.6%). "About a third" understates it by roughly five percentage points. Harmless, but if the number appears again in Chapter 15 it should be consistent. **Recommendation: "about four in ten,"** which is closer.

### 4.5 Not in the source, and I want to be sure I did not imply it

I wrote that the asymptomatic volunteers were "not in remission, and they had not learned to live with it." That is my inference from the study design — they were people *without* back pain, screened as such — not a statement the paper makes about how they were recruited or whether any had prior episodes. **It is a fair reading of "asymptomatic," but it is my gloss, and if the study's inclusion criteria were narrower than I assume, the sentence is slightly stronger than warranted.** Verifying the full text would settle it. Low risk, but logged.

### 4.6 The 96%-at-eighty figure and how readers hear it

Not an error, but a reading risk. "96 per cent of eighty-year-olds" invites two opposite misreadings: *"so it doesn't matter"* and *"so I'm doomed."* The chapter's counterweight handles the first. **It does not explicitly handle the second**, and I think it should — one sentence, somewhere in the scan section, making clear that a common finding is not a predestined outcome. Logged for the revision pass.
