### 1.2 IASP paraphrase — accuracy verification

You asked me to verify the paraphrase actually reflects the source. Here is the check, clause by clause.

**Official wording:** *"An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage."*

| Element of the source | My paraphrase | Accurate? |
|---|---|---|
| *unpleasant* | "unpleasant" | ✅ retained — this word is doing analytical work in the paragraph that follows, and it is a common English adjective rather than distinctive IASP phrasing |
| *sensory and emotional experience* | "an unpleasant experience that combines sensation and emotion" | ✅ The sense is preserved; the phrasing is restructured. "Sensory" → "sensation", "emotional" → "emotion", "experience" retained because there is no synonym that carries it |
| *associated with … actual or potential tissue damage* | "arises in connection with tissue that is actually damaged or potentially at risk of damage" | ✅ "associated with" → "arises in connection with"; "actual or potential" → "actually damaged or potentially at risk of damage" |
| *or resembling that associated with* | "or with something that closely resembles it" | ✅ Preserved. This is the clause most often dropped, and it matters — it is what makes the definition able to account for pain without tissue damage |
| *(attribution)* | "The International Association for the Study of Pain, whose definitions are the ones most clinicians work from" | ✅ Named, and the claim about clinicians following it is accurate — the 2020 revision was developed with IASP membership and is the standard reference |

**Assessment: the paraphrase is faithful.** It preserves all four semantic elements, drops no qualifier, and adds no mechanism that the source does not contain. It is longer than the original, which is the normal cost of paraphrasing a definition this compressed.

**One risk I want on the record:** a paraphrase of a *definition* will always track the definition closely, because the definition has no surplus meaning to discard. The sentence "combines sensation and emotion" is my wording, but any accurate paraphrase of this definition will necessarily say something very close to it. **This is as far from the source's phrasing as an accurate paraphrase can get**, and I think that is the correct place to stop.

## 2. EDITORIAL SELF-REVIEW

### 2.1 The revised IASP passage — does it still work?

**Honest answer: it is slightly worse prose and considerably safer legally, and I think that is the right trade.**

The direct quotation had a rhythm that the paraphrase cannot match — "an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience" is a tight, memorable phrase, and "an unpleasant experience that combines sensation and emotion" is not. But the paragraph's real work was never in the quotation. It was in the sentence that follows it ("*Experience.* Not damage."), and that sentence still lands, because the paraphrase still puts *experience* at the centre.

**The one thing I lost:** quoting the definition signalled that this is a real, citable, institutional definition rather than my characterisation of one. Named attribution does most of that work back — but a reader skimming will now see "the IASP describes pain as…" and take it slightly more on trust. That is a small cost and an acceptable one.

**What I gained:** the paragraph can now be quoted, excerpted, and reviewed without a permissions question attached to it. For a book that will be excerpted in marketing copy, that matters more than the rhythm.

### 2.2 The shortened "not saying" section

Cut from ~200 words to 132. What went:

- **"The body matters. Load matters. Sleep matters. What you do with your arms above your head for eight hours a day matters."** — four short sentences in a row, which is a rhetorical device I overuse, and three of the four are restated in the next sentence anyway.
- **"That is not what the evidence says, and it is not what this book teaches."** — redundant with the following paragraph.
- **"Most of what follows is an attempt to work usefully inside that looseness rather than to resolve it."** — this was the best sentence in the section and I nearly kept it. It went because the section's job is now done by the paragraph above it, and keeping the good sentence would have meant keeping the warm-up sentences that set it up.

What stayed, because it carries information found nowhere else in the chapter: the specific misreading ("it's all nervous system"), the correction (tissue state is one input, and the one most responsive to what you do), and the reframing of the book's position as uncomfortable rather than resolved.

**My judgment: it still earns its place.** It is the only thing standing between Chapter 1 and a reader concluding that the body is irrelevant — a misreading that would make Chapters 3 and 13 incoherent. But it is now closer to the line where I would cut it, and if layout is tight at Stage 7, this is the section to sacrifice.

### 2.3 Corrections made — including one that went beyond the source

You asked me to correct statements going beyond what the source establishes. Three, and the first is the one that matters.

**1. I removed a claim I had no basis for.** The previous draft said the asymptomatic volunteers "were not in remission, and they had not learned to live with it." That is not in the abstract. It is a reasonable inference from the word *asymptomatic*, but I was asserting facts about how those 3,110 people were recruited and what their history was, and I do not know either. **Cut.** The sentence now simply says they had no back pain and their spines looked like that.

This is the exact failure mode the corrections were aimed at: a plausible inference, in a direction that supported my argument, stated as fact. I would rather you saw it than not.

**2. Annular fissures — cut entirely,** as you directed. "Followed the same curve" implied a steep age gradient; the actual figures (19% → 29%) rise gently, while degeneration goes 37→96 and bulge 30→84. The sentence was loose *and* added nothing, since two dramatic figures make the point better than three mixed ones.

**3. "About a third" → "about four in ten."** The actual figure is 38.6% asymptomatic. "About a third" understated it by roughly five points. Corrected.

**4. Added one sentence to the scan section** (not requested, so flagging it): "a finding this common is not a sentence." Reading the section without it, "96 per cent of eighty-year-olds" invites *"so I'm doomed"* as readily as *"so it doesn't matter,"* and the chapter only handled the second. This is a small addition and it is the kind of line that prevents a reader from misusing the book's own honesty against themselves.

### 2.5 The remaining long sentences — my honest position

You told me to split the 52-word sentence and to prioritize readability over stylistic cleverness. Done. But my 35-word rule is now flagging eight sentences at 37–43 words, and I have not split them. I want to say why rather than let it look like an oversight.

Seven of the eight are **colon-and-list** sentences — a short setup followed by a colon and a three-part enumeration ("here is how today is, here is what I can do, and here is when to stop"). These read as fast as short sentences because the commas do the parsing work, and splitting them into separate sentences would produce a choppy, mechanical rhythm that reads *worse* aloud. One is a sentence with two em-dash interruptions, which is a legitimate structure for a parenthetical aside.

**I think the 35-word threshold is the wrong instrument for these seven,** and applying it mechanically would damage the prose. But that is a judgment call, not a compliance result, so: **if you want a hard 35-word ceiling, say so and I will split all eight.** The cost is a noticeably more staccato chapter.
