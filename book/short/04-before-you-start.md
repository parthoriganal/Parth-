# 4 · Before You Start

This is the chapter to read first, and it is deliberately placed before any instruction.

A book cannot examine you. I cannot feel where your pain is, ask what makes it worse, notice that your gait is asymmetric, or take a history. Everything in the following chapter is written for a general reader with a common, non-specific musculoskeletal complaint, and it is entirely possible that you are not that reader. This chapter is how you find out.

## The things that need a clinician, not a ball

Start with the short list. These are reasons to seek assessment rather than to begin self-treatment, and some are reasons to seek it today.

**Go to an emergency department or call emergency services the same day if you have:**

- Numbness in the saddle area — between the legs, around the genitals or anus
- New difficulty passing urine, inability to pass urine, incontinence, or loss of bowel control
- Weakness or numbness in both legs
- A calf that is swollen, hot, red, and tender, particularly if it came on suddenly
- Sudden severe neck pain or a headache unlike any you have had, especially with neck movement
- New dizziness, double vision, slurred speech, facial numbness, or difficulty swallowing, particularly after moving your neck or after a neck injury
- Chest pain, breathlessness, or pain brought on by exertion
- A fever with severe back pain, or unexplained fever alongside new spinal pain

The first three on that list together describe the classic presentation of cauda equina syndrome, a compression of the nerve roots below the spinal cord. A 2025 review of international guidelines found that perineal sensory change and bladder or bowel dysfunction appear in all of the guideline documents examined, with radicular pain in most and new motor weakness in about half. All agree that suspected cauda equina syndrome requires urgent imaging. It is rare. It is also one of the few situations in musculoskeletal care where a delay of days rather than hours can change the outcome permanently, which is why it is at the top of a book about tennis balls.

**See a clinician within days, not weeks, if you have:**

- Pain that has worsened steadily over two to three weeks despite sensible activity
- Pain that consistently wakes you at night
- Unexplained weight loss
- New back or bone pain with a history of cancer
- Pain after a significant fall, collision, or accident
- Progressive stiffness accompanied by systemic symptoms such as fever or malaise
- Any symptom that frightens you, even if you cannot say why

## Why this list is not a warning about you

There is a failure mode in writing about red flags, and it runs in two directions.

The first is to present the list as a threat, which produces a reader who is now afraid of their own back and less able to move, which is the opposite of useful. The second is to attach a reassuring statistic so that the list can be waved away, which is how a delayed diagnosis happens.

Neither is honest, so here is the accurate version. In one large study of 1,172 people who consulted a family doctor with acute low back pain, 11 — just under one per cent — turned out to have a serious spinal condition, mostly vertebral fractures. But 80 per cent of that whole group had at least one warning sign, and the doctors missed more than half of the serious cases at the first visit.

Two things follow from those numbers, and they point in opposite directions. Warning signs are common and they are not a diagnosis; having one does not mean something is seriously wrong. And warning signs are not a substitute for judgement, which is why the instruction is to get checked rather than to assess yourself. That study concerned acute low back pain in primary care. It tells you nothing about neck pain, nothing about long-standing pain, and nothing about anyone already under specialist care.

## Conditions that change the rules

The following are not necessarily reasons to avoid all self-treatment, but they change what is safe, and several are absolute exclusions for particular techniques.

| Condition | What changes |
|---|---|
| **Pregnancy** | Avoid lying on your back after the first trimester; avoid abdominal pressure; ask your midwife or clinician before starting |
| **Anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication; bleeding disorders** | Bruising risk is higher; use substantially less pressure, and get advice first |
| **Reduced sensation** — peripheral neuropathy, diabetes with neuropathy, spinal cord injury | **Do not use pressure you cannot feel accurately.** The feedback loop that makes this safe is absent |
| **Severe osteoporosis** | Avoid loading over bone; no pressure on the spine |
| **Recent surgery** | No work on or near the site until cleared by the surgeon |
| **Known or suspected deep vein thrombosis** | **Absolute exclusion in the affected limb** |
| **Peripheral arterial disease** | Avoid calf pressure |
| **Varicose veins** | Avoid direct pressure on the veins |
| **Active cancer** | Get advice before any technique |
| **Hypermobility spectrum disorder or hEDS** | Reduce end-range work; prioritise control over range |
| **Acute injury, bruising, swelling, open wound, or skin infection** | Avoid the area entirely |
| **Fever or acute illness** | Defer until you are well |

The international expert consensus on contraindications to myofascial work is unusually clear on a few of these. Local tissue inflammation drew 97 per cent agreement as a contraindication, deep vein thrombosis 97 per cent, bone infection 94 per cent, myositis ossificans 92 per cent, fracture 84 per cent, and open wounds 73 per cent. Those figures are the closest thing this field has to a settled position on safety.

## The rule that governs everything else

If you take one instruction from this chapter, take this one.

**When in doubt, don't.**

The asymmetry is stark. If you skip a session you were unsure about, you have lost fifteen minutes. If you proceed with one you should have skipped, you may lose considerably more. A book cannot examine you, which means the judgement has to sit with you, and the correct default when that judgement is uncertain is not to act.

There is a second rule, equally short, that will appear again in the routine: **pain is a stop signal, not a target.** The reasons are in the next chapter, but the practice is simple. Sharp pain, pain that travels down a limb, numbness, tingling, weakness, dizziness, or a burning sensation all mean stop. Soreness afterwards that lasts a few hours is common; a flare that carries into the next day means less pressure and less time, not more.

## What this chapter can and cannot do

It can tell you when to stop and when to seek help. It can tell you which conditions make specific techniques unsafe. It can give you a list that a careful clinician would want you to know.

It cannot examine you, and it cannot diagnose you. If you finish this chapter unsure whether the routine is appropriate for you, that uncertainty is the answer: ask someone qualified before you begin. A physiotherapist, a GP, or a specialist musculoskeletal clinician can answer in ten minutes a question this book can only approach in fifteen pages.

One last thing, which matters more than it may seem. Nothing in this chapter, and nothing anywhere in this book, implies that your pain is not real, that it is your fault, or that you should have to manage it alone. Self-treatment is one small option among several. For some readers it will be a useful part of a larger plan. For others the right answer is professional care, and for some it is care plus time plus nothing else for a while. All of those are legitimate outcomes, and none of them is a failure.
