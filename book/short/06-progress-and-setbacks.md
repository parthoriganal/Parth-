# 6 · Progress, Setbacks, and Knowing When to Stop

Two weeks of doing this, and you will want to know whether it is working. The honest answer is that you will not be able to tell from how you feel, because memory of pain is unreliable and there is a strong human tendency to notice the bad days and let the good ones pass unrecorded.

So don't rely on your impressions. Record a small amount, consistently.

## The four things worth recording

**One measure you can repeat.** How far you can turn your head, how far you can reach down, how long you can sit before it starts. Measure it the same way, at the same time of day, against something fixed. This is the most informative thing on the page, and it is the thing most people leave out.

**One notice-rating, once a day.** A single 0–10 number at the same hour, answering "how much did this bother me today." Not your worst moment, not your best. One number.

**What you did.** Which regions, roughly how long, and whether you pushed.

**Whether the next day was worse.** This is the only safety signal you have between sessions, and it is the one that changes the plan.

Leave out everything else. Hourly pain scores, detailed sleep logs, and notes on every twinge produce so much noise that the signal disappears, and tending the record becomes a source of anxiety in its own right.

## How to read it without fooling yourself

Look at direction over two weeks, not at individual days. Ignore single bad days entirely; everybody has them and they do not mean anything. Be suspicious, in both directions, of a good week that followed a holiday or a bad week that followed an argument, because those are the weeks where your expectations are doing the work.

And hold one distinction firmly, because this is where expectations go wrong. There is a difference between immediate relief and lasting improvement. Relief after pressure is the reliable part: it arrives within minutes and lasts from a few hours to a day. Lasting improvement takes weeks, requires load and repetition, and is much less reliable. A technique that makes you feel better for an hour is not the same as a treatment that changes your trajectory, and no practice in this book can promise the second one.

Statistical significance deserves a note here too, since it is easy to misread. A trial can report that a difference was unlikely to be chance, and the people in it can still report feeling no different. Both statements can be true at once, because significance is about whether an effect exists and clinical importance is about whether it is big enough to notice. When this book has said the evidence is moderate, that is what it means: probably real, modest in size, and not a guarantee about you.

## Setbacks

Setbacks are normal, and their shape is predictable enough to be worth describing.

**A flare after a session** means one of two things: too much pressure, or too much time. Reduce one of them and see what happens. Do not reduce both at once, or you will not learn which it was.

**A flare that arrived on its own,** unrelated to anything you did, is not evidence that the practice is failing. Recurrence of pain is a feature of most long-standing musculoskeletal conditions and does not indicate a new injury.

**A plateau** is not failure either. The early changes come fast, because a good deal of what changes early is guarding and confidence. What follows is slower, and the flat part of the curve is where most people stop.

**A worsening week** is the one to take seriously if it is genuinely persistent. Two to three weeks of steady worsening despite sensible activity, or any new neurological sign, means the routine goes on hold and you get assessed. Not because something terrible is likely, but because that pattern is the reason assessments exist.

## When to stop altogether

Three legitimate reasons to stop, and none of them is failure.

**It is not helping and it is not neutral.** If four to six weeks of consistent practice has produced no change in your recorded measure and no change in how much the problem interferes with your life, the honest conclusion is that this is not the tool for you. Put it down.

**It is making things worse.** Recurrent flares that follow sessions, increasing soreness, or any neurological symptom mean stop and get advice.

**Something in chapter four applies.** A new symptom from the emergency list, a new condition, a change in medication, a pregnancy. The screening list is not a one-time gate.

## The decision at two weeks

At the end of the fortnight you have three options, and they are all defensible.

**Continue as you are** if the recorded measure is moving in the right direction, the next-day signal is neutral or positive, and the practice is not costing you more than it gives.

**Reduce it** if you are seeing some benefit but the practice has become a chore, or if the movement block is the part that is working and the body work is not. There is no rule that says you must do all fifteen minutes.

**Stop and seek help** if none of the above applies, or if anything in chapter four has changed.

## On expectation, honestly

Expectation is not a weakness and it is not nothing. Believing that something will help can produce real relief, through mechanisms that are ordinary and well recognised. It is not evidence that the practice is doing something structural, but it is also not a reason to dismiss what you feel. If two weeks of this makes your neck feel better and you cannot fully explain why, the sensible response is to keep doing the part that helps and to remain sceptical of explanations that go further than the evidence does.

That is an unusual position to be asked to hold, and it is the one this book keeps returning to. You can use something without overstating it. You can notice benefit without having to claim a mechanism. The alternative — needing the story to be grand in order for the practice to count — is what turns a useful fifteen minutes into a belief system, and belief systems are harder to put down when they stop working.

One more thing about the two weeks, and then we are nearly done. If you notice that the act of recording is making you more attentive to your body in a way that is unhelpful — more worried, more vigilant, more inclined to check — then stop recording. The point of the exercise is information, not surveillance. Some people find that a weekly note serves them better than a daily one. That is a fine adaptation and not a sign that you are doing it wrong.

## What progress actually looks like

Not a straight line, and not a dramatic one. In practice it looks like this: the bad days are less bad; the measure you chose has moved a few degrees or a few centimetres; you notice the problem less often because you are thinking about it less; and the fifteen minutes have stopped being a task and become something you do while the kettle boils.

If that is what two weeks gives you, it is enough. It is not a cure and nobody promised one. It is the difference between a body you are negotiating with and a body you are managing, and for most people with a long-standing stiff neck or aching hip, that is the thing actually worth having.
