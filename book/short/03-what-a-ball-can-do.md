# 3 · What a Ball Can and Cannot Do

Press your thumb into the sore part of your shoulder and hold it there. Something happens. The tissue gives a little, the sensation changes, and when you let go the area feels different from how it did.

The interesting question is what, exactly, just happened — and this is where a great deal of confident writing goes wrong, in both directions. The enthusiastic answer is that you have broken up adhesions and released restricted fascia. The dismissive answer is that you have done nothing at all. Neither is right.

## The force problem

Start with the physics, because it is the cleanest part.

To permanently deform collagenous tissue, you need to apply load that produces elongation in the range of several per cent, sustained. This is well established in tissue mechanics, and it is the basis of how ligaments and tendons respond to stretching and immobilisation. Several per cent of the length of a collagen bundle is a great deal more than it sounds.

Now consider what your thumb, or a tennis ball, or your body weight on a foam roller, can deliver through intact skin, subcutaneous fat, and the muscle layer above the tissue you are aiming at. The force is spread over a broad area, partly absorbed by everything in between, and applied for seconds to minutes. It cannot generate that degree of elongation in a deep collagen structure.

This is not a controversial claim, and it is the one serious question to ask of any book promising to restructure your tissue: by what mechanism, at what force, sustained for how long? The answer is usually that the author has not asked.

So a ball does not break up adhesions. It does not lengthen restricted fascia. It does not "release" a structure you could name. If a practitioner tells you they have done so, they have either misread the literature or are using language loosely enough that it cannot be checked.

## What probably is happening

The honest answer is more interesting than the false one, and it explains why the practice feels like something.

Pressure on tissue produces local changes in blood flow. It alters the input arriving at sensory nerve endings. It changes how the area feels to move immediately afterwards, and it can reduce the guarding that has been protecting an area — the involuntary bracing that accompanies pain. There is a plausible neurological account involving the modulation of afferent input and a reduction in protective muscle activity, and it fits the observed time course far better than a mechanical one does.

Which is to say: the effect is real, it is largely temporary, and it is mostly in how the area feels and moves rather than in what the tissue is made of. A 2026 review of the field put the general point about the shape of the clinical evidence succinctly, noting that reduced fascial shear strain in low back pain has not replicated across studies.

This is not a consolation prize. A temporary reduction in guarding and an area that feels better to move is exactly what you need in order to move it, and moving it is where the durable benefit lives. That ordering matters, and we will come back to it at the end of the book: this tool makes movement tolerable. It does not substitute for it.

## What the trials actually show

Two bodies of evidence are worth knowing, and their quality matters as much as their direction.

For chronic low back pain, a 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled eight randomised controlled trials covering 375 people. Myofascial release produced statistically significant improvement in pain and in physical function. Certainty was rated **moderate** for those two outcomes — meaning further research would probably not change the estimate much, but the estimate is not strong. The same review found **no** significant effect on quality of life, mental health, pressure pain threshold, or trunk mobility, and rated certainty for those outcomes as very low or low.

For chronic neck pain, a 2023 meta-analysis pooled ten trials and 549 people. It found a significant reduction in pain, with a reported effect size of 0.86, and improvements in two ranges of motion. Certainty was limited by the fact that almost none of the trials could be blinded — you cannot conceal from a participant whether someone is pressing on them — and by small samples.

Read those two paragraphs and then look at how the category is marketed. The evidence says moderate, narrow, short-term benefit on two outcomes in low back pain, and a probable short-term pain benefit in neck pain, in unblinded trials. That is worth having. It is not a cure, and it is not what "release your fascia and end chronic pain" promises.

## Does it beat exercise?

This is the question the shelf avoids, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a rhetorical one.

The most robust relevant evidence comes from a 2021 Cochrane review of exercise for chronic low back pain. It found **moderate-certainty** evidence that exercise reduces pain compared with no treatment, usual care, or placebo — a mean difference of about 15 points on a 100-point scale, which meets the threshold for a clinically important difference. It also found that when exercise was compared with other conservative treatments, the differences were small and not clinically important. And in a subgroup analysis, exercise was better than advice or education alone, and better than electrotherapy, but **no difference was observed when exercise was compared with manual therapy.**

That last finding is the important one, and it is routinely misreported. It does not show that manual therapy, including self-applied pressure, is as good as exercise. It shows that in the trials where the two were compared directly, neither came out ahead. A network meta-analysis of eleven trials in chronic neck pain found every intervention beat doing nothing, and that manual therapy's point estimate was larger than exercise alone — but the estimates in that analysis were all constructed against a control group rather than measured head-to-head, they concerned pain only, and roughly three-quarters of the included trials carried some or high risk of bias.

Put together, the defensible reading is this. Doing something beats doing nothing. Combining the two is better supported than either alone. There is no reliable evidence that pressure work outperforms exercise, and the best single piece of direct evidence available — the Cochrane subgroup — found no difference between them.

What follows for you is a specific and slightly uncomfortable conclusion: **if you only had time for one thing, it would not be the ball.** That is not a reason to skip the ball. It is a reason to stop thinking of it as the treatment and start thinking of it as the part that makes the treatment possible.

## The trigger point problem

One more piece of vocabulary needs clearing, because it is load-bearing for most of the books in this category.

The "trigger point" is a tender spot in muscle held to cause local and referred pain, and it has a large clinical literature behind it. It also has a serious problem, which is reliability. A 2009 systematic review of the diagnostic examination found that no study had reported inter-rater reliability for identifying the location of active trigger points in symptomatic people, and concluded that physical examination could not be recommended as a reliable test for their diagnosis. In 2014, a review in *Rheumatology* went further, arguing that the construct had been refuted — while being careful to say that the clinical phenomena patients describe are real and are better explained by known neurophysiology. That argument was rebutted in print the following year by researchers who considered it out of date and pointed to newer reliability work.

The dispute is live and I am not going to adjudicate it. What follows practically is simple. In this book I will say "an area that feels tight or tender," because that is what you can actually identify. I will not tell you that you have a trigger point, because neither you nor I can establish that with any reliability, and because the label implies a diagnosis that would need a clinician.

## What you are left with

A tool that reliably changes how an area feels for a while, with moderate evidence of short-term benefit on pain and function in two chronic conditions, no evidence that it restructures tissue, and no evidence that it outperforms movement. That is a modest instrument, honestly described.

It is also, held in the right place in a routine, a genuinely useful one. The next chapter is about the circumstances in which you should not use it at all.
