# 7 · Quick Reference

The book is short by design, and this chapter is the part you will use. Everything below is compressed from the chapters before it, and nothing here is new.

## The rules

Work at three or four out of ten. Short holds of twenty to sixty seconds, a few times. One to three minutes per area. Stop on any sharp pain, radiating pain, numbness, tingling, weakness, dizziness, or burning. Soreness for a few hours is normal; a flare into the next day means less pressure and less time. When in doubt, don't.

## Stop now, and get emergency help, if you have

Saddle numbness. New bladder or bowel problems, including difficulty passing urine or loss of control. Weakness or numbness in both legs. A calf that is swollen, hot, red, and tender. Sudden severe neck pain or a new severe headache, particularly with neck movement. New dizziness, double vision, slurred speech, facial numbness, or difficulty swallowing. Chest pain or breathlessness. Fever with severe back pain.

## See a clinician within days if you have

Pain worsening steadily over two to three weeks. Night pain that wakes you. Unexplained weight loss. New spinal pain with a history of cancer. Pain after a significant fall or collision. Stiffness with systemic symptoms. Any symptom that frightens you.

## Do not apply pressure if you have

Reduced sensation you cannot feel accurately. Known or suspected deep vein thrombosis, or calf risk factors for one. Skin infection, open wound, acute bruising, or swelling in the area. A recent fracture or surgery in the area. Severe osteoporosis, over bone or spine. Active cancer, without advice first. Fever or acute illness.

Use substantially less pressure, and ask first, if you take anticoagulants or antiplatelets, are pregnant, or have a bleeding disorder or hypermobility spectrum condition.

## Where not to press

Front and sides of the neck. Directly over the cervical spine. The base of the skull with pressure. Over the spinous processes anywhere. The axilla. Directly over the sciatic path in the deep gluteal region. The head of the fibula. Directly on varicose veins. The calf, if any thrombosis risk factor is present.

## The fifteen minutes

Two minutes settling and breathing. Nine minutes of body work, two or three regions, at three or four out of ten. Four minutes of movement, which is the part that matters. Five-minute version on bad weeks: one region, one movement.

## The weekly template

Neck-adjacent and upper back on Monday, Wednesday, Friday. Hip and gluteal on Tuesday and Saturday. Hip and thigh on Thursday. Sunday off or a walk. This is one arrangement, not a prescription; what matters is that you rotate rather than repeat, and that you do not attempt everything in one session.

## The four movements

Neck rotation, slowly, within comfort. Scapular setting — draw the shoulder blades back and down without shrugging. Hip hinge — push the hips back with a soft knee and a long spine. Walking, to a distance you would do on an ordinary day.

## Two weeks of noticing

One repeatable measure, taken the same way at the same time. One 0–10 rating once a day. What you did. Whether the next day was worse. Read the direction over a fortnight, ignore single bad days, and decide at the end: continue, reduce, or stop and get advice.

## If nothing changes

Four to six weeks of consistent practice with no movement in your recorded measure and no change in how much the problem interferes with your life is a clear answer. Stop, and spend the time on something else. That is a good outcome, arrived at honestly, and it is better than another year of a practice that is not working.

## What this book was, and what it was not

It was an attempt to describe one small, cheap, low-risk practice accurately, and to be clear about the limits of what it can do.

It was not a cure, and it does not contain one. It did not claim that pressure restructures your fascia, breaks up adhesions, or releases anything that could be named; chapter three explains why those claims do not hold. It did not tell you that your pain is imaginary if your scan is normal, and it did not tell you that your scan explains your pain. It did not suggest that self-treatment replaces assessment, and it did not offer a diagnosis of any kind.

Three things are worth carrying out of it.

The first is that pain is real and produced rather than simply received, which means your experience is not in question even when its cause is uncertain. The second is that the strongest evidence in this territory concerns movement, not pressure, and the ball's honest job is to make movement tolerable. The third is that the judgement about when to stop belongs to you, and that exercising it is not a failure of the practice but part of it.

If the book has done its work, the morning check has changed slightly. It is still there — I still do mine — but it is no longer a verdict delivered before you are fully awake. It is a report from a system you understand a little better than you did, followed by a decision you are equipped to make.

That is a modest thing. It is also, as far as the evidence goes, the true one.
