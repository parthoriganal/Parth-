# 5 · The Three Tools, and the Routine

Before any technique, three principles. They are the whole of the method, and everything in this chapter is an application of them.

**Work at three or four out of ten.** Not five, not seven. Find it the first time by starting at almost nothing and increasing slowly until you can feel it clearly and could comfortably fall asleep. That is three.

**Short holds, repeated.** Twenty to sixty seconds, a few times, with breathing. One to three minutes per area in total. There is no validated dose for any of this; those numbers are a practical starting point, not a finding from a trial, and I would rather say so than dress them up.

**Stop on any signal.** Sharp pain, pain travelling down an arm or leg, numbness, tingling, weakness, dizziness, visual change, or a burning sensation. And the next-day rule: soreness for a few hours is normal, a flare that lasts into the next day means less pressure and less time.

## Why gentler is not the timid option

The instinct to press harder when something isn't working is nearly universal, and it is worth understanding why it fails.

Pain modulates pain. Pressing hard enough to hurt produces a nociceptive input of its own, and the nervous system responds by increasing protective activity in the area — the guarding you were trying to reduce. You have not opened a window; you have prompted the tissue to defend itself. Meanwhile the sensation is intense enough to occupy your attention, which many people read as effectiveness. It is not. Intensity is a measure of how much stimulation you applied, not of how much useful change occurred.

A harder ball concentrates force. A tennis ball is about 58 millimetres across and deforms under load, which spreads the force over a wider area and limits how much pressure any one point can receive. That compliance is a safety feature, not a compromise. A lacrosse ball, a golf ball, or anything with knobs concentrates force into a small area over structures you cannot see, and it does so for no demonstrated additional benefit. I am not going to recommend them.

## The three tools

**The tennis ball** is the workhorse. Small enough to be specific, soft enough to be self-limiting, cheap enough to own several.

**The wall** is the most underrated part of the kit. Working against a wall lets you control the amount of force with your legs rather than with your arms, which means you can back off instantly by straightening a knee. It also makes the lower back and shoulders accessible without lying on the floor with your full body weight on a ball.

**The towel** does two jobs. Rolled, it changes the angle of a stretch or supports a limb so that you can relax into it. Folded, it can provide gentle traction that a ball cannot.

What you do not need: a foam roller is useful for broad areas and is not dangerous, but it is large and difficult to control near the spine, and it is not necessary for anything in this book. A massage ball is a reasonable substitute for the tennis ball if it is similarly compliant.

## The routine

Fifteen minutes, three blocks. Two minutes to settle, nine minutes of body work, four minutes of movement. The movement block is not optional and is the part that matters most; I will explain why at the end.

Do two or three regions per session, not all of them. The weekly template below rotates so that no two consecutive days repeat the same pair.

| Day | Regions | Movement |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Neck-adjacent and upper back | Neck rotation, scapular setting |
| Tuesday | Hip and gluteal | Hip hinge, hip rotation |
| Wednesday | Upper back and shoulders | Thoracic extension, scapular setting |
| Thursday | Hip and thigh | Hip rotation, walking |
| Friday | Neck-adjacent and upper back | Neck rotation, scapular setting |
| Saturday | Hip and gluteal | Hip hinge, walking |
| Sunday | Nothing, or five minutes | Walk |

## Working around the neck, not on it

This is the section where the book's caution is least negotiable, so I will put the rule before the technique.

**Do not manipulate your own neck.** No thrusting, no forcing it to end range, no trying to make it crack. A 2026 systematic review of adverse events following cervical self-manipulation identified 24 reported cases. Seventy-one per cent were male, the mean age was 34, and half had neck pain or stiffness beforehand — which is to say, these were people doing what you might be tempted to do. Twelve of the 24 were severe or catastrophic, and two were fatal. Twenty-two of the 24 occurred within two days. Fifteen involved the person's own hands. Most of the events were vascular.

Those are case reports, the weakest form of evidence, and the true incidence is genuinely unknown — a systematic review of the association between cervical manipulation and carotid dissection found no epidemiological study that measured it. So I am not telling you this will happen to you. I am telling you that the manoeuvre has no benefit that would justify even a small risk, which is why this book does not teach it.

What you do instead is work the tissue *around* the neck. Upper trapezius with the ball against the wall, at the shoulder rather than at the neck. The upper medial border of the scapula, for the muscles that attach there. The thoracic spine with a rolled towel, because a great deal of what people experience as neck stiffness originates in the upper back, which is a different structure with a different risk profile.

Avoid entirely: the front and sides of the neck, where the carotid arteries and airway are; the back of the neck directly over the cervical spine; the base of the skull with pressure; and anything that reproduces dizziness, visual change, or nausea.

## Hips and glutes

The gluteal region tolerates more pressure than the neck, and responds well to a ball used seated or against a wall. Place the ball under one buttock, lean back, and control the load by how much weight you take through that side. Work the outer and upper part.

Two cautions. The sciatic nerve runs deep through this region; sustained deep pressure there is not appropriate, and any reproduction of leg pain, tingling, or numbness is a stop signal rather than a target to work through. And the iliotibial band — the thick band down the outside of the thigh — cannot be stretched or broken down by pressure, and heavy direct work on it is uncomfortable and mechanically pointless. Work the muscle around it instead: gluteus medius at the hip, vastus lateralis on the outer thigh.

Hip flexors respond better to a towel than a ball. Lie on your side with a rolled towel under the hip, or use a supported half-kneeling position and let the hip settle rather than forcing it.

## Lower back, thighs, feet

**The lower back is where self-treatment most often becomes self-injury.** The rule is no direct pressure on the spine or over the spinous processes, and no lying on a ball on the floor with your full body weight. Where you work is the paraspinal muscle either side of the spine, with the ball against a wall so that your legs control the load. There is also a mechanical reason for the caution that is worth knowing: the thoracolumbar fascia is thick and largely not accessible to a ball in any case, so the dramatic version of this technique was never doing what it claimed.

**Calf work carries one absolute rule.** If you have any risk factor for deep vein thrombosis — recent surgery, prolonged immobility, long-haul travel, a clotting disorder, active cancer, pregnancy, or oestrogen therapy — do not apply pressure to the calf. A calf that is unilaterally swollen, warm, red, and tender needs urgent assessment, not massage.

**Thighs** are forgiving. Quadriceps and hamstrings with a ball or roller, broad and moderate, avoiding the outer knee and the head of the fibula where the common peroneal nerve runs.

**Feet** are the safest and most reliably pleasant region in the book. Seated, ball under the arch, control the weight through your leg. Nothing bad happens here, which makes it a reasonable place to start if you are uncertain about the rest.

## The five-minute version

There will be weeks when fifteen minutes is not available. The reduced version is one region and one movement, five minutes, and it is not a failure state. It is the maintenance dose, and it is better than nothing precisely because it survives a bad week.

## The part that matters

Here is the honest accounting. If you only had time for one thing, it would not be the ball. The strongest evidence in this book concerns exercise: a Cochrane review found moderate-certainty evidence that exercise reduces chronic low back pain compared with no treatment, and no difference when exercise was compared directly with manual therapy. For neck pain, the evidence favours strengthening rather than stretching, and the guidance from NICE on low back pain considers manual therapy including soft-tissue work only as part of a treatment package that includes exercise.

That last phrase describes the ball's real role exactly. It is the part that makes the area comfortable enough to move, and the movement is what changes the trajectory. Which means the four minutes at the end of the routine are not a warm-down. They are the point, and the nine minutes before them are what allow you to do them.

Four movements, none of them requiring equipment: neck rotation within comfort, a scapular setting exercise, a hip hinge, and walking. Keep them unremarkable. Nothing in this book requires you to be athletic, and the best movement is the one you will still be doing in six weeks.
