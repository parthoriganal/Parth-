# 1 · The Morning Check

Before you get out of bed, you check.

Most people with a stiff neck or an aching hip do this without deciding to. You turn your head a little against the pillow and wait for the answer. You roll one shoulder back and see how far it goes. You notice, in the first conscious second of the day, whether today is a good day or one of the other kind. Then you get up and arrange the morning around what you found.

It is a strange habit when you look at it directly. You are consulting your body about a day that hasn't happened yet. But it makes sense, because the answer changes things — what you reach for, how you sit, whether you take the stairs, whether you say yes to something in the afternoon. A stiff neck at seven in the morning is not only a sensation. It is a forecast, and you plan around it.

This book is about that moment. Not about erasing it, which no book can do. It is about changing what the moment means. Right now, for most people, the morning check is a verdict: something is wrong, and you would like to know how wrong. I would like it to become information instead. Here is how today is. Here is what I might do about it. Here is when I should stop and ask somebody who can examine me.

That is a smaller promise than the ones you have probably been offered. It is also the only one I can keep, and I will spend much of this book explaining why the larger promises do not survive contact with the evidence.

## What pain is, and what it is not

There is a definition most of the medical world works from, and it is worth knowing because it is more careful than the way we usually talk about pain.

The International Association for the Study of Pain describes pain as an unpleasant experience that combines sensation and emotion. On their account it arises in connection with tissue that is actually damaged or at risk of damage, or with something that closely resembles that situation. Notice where the weight falls. Not on the damage. On the experience. The definition places pain among the things that happen to a person — fear, hunger, grief are nearby — rather than among the things that happen to a tissue, like a bruise or a fracture.

The association adds a second point in the notes attached to that definition, and it is the one that matters most for everything that follows. Pain and nociception are different things. Nociception is the nervous system's process of detecting and encoding potentially damaging stimuli: a physical, measurable event. Pain is what a person experiences. You cannot determine how much pain someone is in by measuring activity in their sensory nerves, because the relationship between the two is not fixed.

The everyday demonstration is a burn. You are occupied, you grab a hot pan, and for a second or two there is no pain at all — just the sight of the burn and the motion of pulling your hand away. Then it arrives. The tissue was damaged at the moment of contact; the pain came later, and it came from your nervous system's interpretation of the event rather than from the event itself. The reverse happens too. A wound everyone agrees should hurt, and for some reason does not very much.

None of this means pain is imaginary. It means pain is produced. Your nervous system assembles it from information about tissue state, but also from attention, expectation, memory, fear, fatigue, and how safe the situation feels. That is not a defect in the system. It is how the system has always worked, and it is why pain can be entirely real while remaining a poor measure of how much damage is present.

One further point from those notes, and it is the rule this book is written under: when a person says they are in pain, what they say should be respected. Not tolerated. Not taken with a grain of salt. Respected. You are the only person with direct access to what you feel, and nothing in the pages that follow is going to suggest that you are mistaken about it.

## The scan problem

If you have had an aching hip or a stiff neck for more than a few months, the chances are good that someone has imaged it, and that the report contained a word — *degeneration*, *bulge*, *tear*, *loss of height* — that sounded like an explanation.

It is worth knowing what those words mean in a body that has no pain at all.

In 2015, a group of researchers pooled 33 studies covering 3,110 people with no back pain whatsoever — volunteers, not patients — and recorded how common these findings were in their spines. Disc degeneration was present in 37 per cent of people in their twenties and 96 per cent of people in their eighties. A disc bulge, the finding that frightens people most, was present in 30 per cent of twenty-year-olds and 84 per cent of eighty-year-olds.

None of these people had back pain. They were simply people whose spines looked like that, in the way most of us will eventually have knees and hips that look like something. And to be clear about the other reading: a finding this common is not a sentence. That does not make the finding invented, and it does not mean it will never matter. It means that on its own it cannot tell you what your back will do next year.

The authors were careful about what their data did and did not show. Their conclusion was that many of these findings probably belong to ordinary ageing rather than to disease, and on their own need not mean pain. They attached a caveat worth keeping: an imaging finding still has to be read alongside the person in front of the clinician, not instead of them.

If you have been carrying a scan result around as though it were a diagnosis, this may be the most useful thing in the book, and I wanted you to have it early. But I am not going to leave it there, because the version of this argument that circulates online is not honest. The same research group published a companion review in the same year, asking whether these findings are *more* common in people who do have back pain. Some of them are, and the pattern is more interesting than a simple yes.

They pooled fourteen studies and 3,097 people aged fifty or under, about four in ten of them without back pain. In that group, disc bulges were substantially more common in people with pain, and so were disc degeneration, disc extrusion and protrusion, Modic 1 changes, and spondylolysis. Other findings showed no clear association at all: Modic changes taken as a whole, central canal stenosis, and the high-intensity zone that appears in so many reports.

So the honest position is not that scans are meaningless. It is narrower and stranger. Some structural findings track with pain in younger adults and some do not, and which is which is not something you can read off your own report.

**Imaging tells you what your spine looks like. It does not tell you what you will feel tomorrow.**

Both halves of that sentence have to be held at once. The finding on your scan is real. It is also extremely common in people who feel fine, and a poor predictor of what any given person will experience. A scan is a photograph of structure; pain is not a structure. It is an event that happens to a person, in a body, in a life.

Which brings me to the sentence I most want to prevent you from hearing, because it is the one people take away from this material and it does real harm: *if your scan is normal, your pain isn't real.* No. A normal scan means the photograph did not show the thing the photograph is good at showing. It does not mean the pain is not happening, and it certainly does not mean there is nothing to do. It means the answer is somewhere the camera cannot see.

## What this chapter is not saying

There is a temptation, on learning that pain and tissue damage are not the same thing, to run with it — to decide that the body doesn't matter, that it is all nervous system, that you could think your way out of this. The evidence does not support that and neither does anything in this book.

Tissue state is one input into pain, and it is the one most responsive to what you do: load, position, how you spend your hours. It is not the only input, and it is not a dial you can turn directly. What follows is therefore less comfortable than either simple story. Your pain is real. Your tissue is real. The relationship between them is looser than "something is damaged" or "it's all in your head" would suggest, and the rest of this book works inside that looseness rather than trying to resolve it.

One consequence is worth stating before we go further, because it shapes everything I will and will not claim later. Because pain is not a readout of tissue state, no self-treatment — no ball, no stretch, no pressure — can be shown to have fixed the thing that was wrong. It can be shown to have changed how you feel. Those are different claims, and most of the fascial-release shelf quietly merges them.
