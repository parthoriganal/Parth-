# Clinician Review Packet

**Manuscript:** *The 15-Minute Fascial Release Plan: Self-Treatment for Stiff Necks, Tight Shoulders, and Aching Hips — Using Only a Tennis Ball, a Wall, and a Towel*

**Manuscript length:** 9,294 body words, 7 chapters. Review scope is ~3,000 words.

**Prepared:** 21 November 2026
**Status:** This book has **not** been clinically reviewed. Nothing in it has been checked by a qualified practitioner. This packet exists so that a clinician can perform that review efficiently.

---

## Note to the reviewing clinician

Thank you for doing this. The book teaches self-applied pressure and movement for neck, shoulder, hip, and lower-back stiffness. It is written for general readers who have not been examined. That is the reason your review matters: the reader cannot be screened by the book, and the book cannot see what you can see.

You are not being asked to endorse the book, the technique, or its evidence. You are being asked one question, applied to each item below:

> **Is anything here likely to cause harm to a reader who follows it in good faith, and is anything here stated with more confidence than the evidence supports?**

**Three response options per item:** *Acceptable as written* / *Acceptable with this change* / *Do not publish this item*.

Please do not soften a finding to be helpful. A flagged item costs an afternoon to fix. A missed one reaches readers who have no way to check it.

**How to record your review:** there is a response table at the end of this document. Alternatively, mark up `PDF-REVIEW/interior-proof.pdf` directly and return that — page references are given throughout and match the proof.

# Part 1 — Safety screening (Chapter 4)

## 1.1 The red-flag tiers

The book presents three tiers. Please check each for **omissions that would be dangerous** and for **over-inclusion that would cause needless alarm**.

| Tier | What the book says | Page |
|---|---|---|
| **Stop now — emergency** | Saddle numbness; new bladder or bowel problems; weakness or numbness in both legs; a calf that is swollen, hot, red, and tender; sudden severe neck pain or new severe headache, particularly with neck movement; new dizziness, double vision, slurred speech, facial numbness, or difficulty swallowing; chest pain or breathlessness; fever with severe back pain | 37 |
| **See a clinician within days** | Pain worsening steadily over two to three weeks; night pain that wakes you; unexplained weight loss; new spinal pain with a history of cancer; pain after a significant fall or collision; stiffness with systemic symptoms; any symptom that frightens you | 37 |
| **Proceed with care / modify** | The contraindication table in 1.2 | 22–23 |

**Clinician questions**

1. Is any item in the emergency tier **wrongly** placed — i.e. something that should be an emergency but is in the "within days" tier?
2. Is any item **missing** that a general reader could plausibly experience and misattribute to muscular stiffness?
3. Specifically: **cauda equina syndrome** is covered via saddle numbness and bladder/bowel change. Is that sufficient for a lay reader who does not know the term?
4. Specifically: **cervical arterial dysfunction**. The book stops the reader from self-manipulating the neck. Does it also adequately warn about the *non*-manipulation signs (new severe headache, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia)? See Chapter 5, page 28.
5. Specifically: **giant cell arteritis / temporal arteritis** is not named. New headache in a reader over 50 is a scenario a self-treatment book will meet. Should it be named in the "within days" tier?

## 1.2 The contraindication table

Thirteen conditions. Please confirm that each "what changes" instruction is clinically correct and sufficiently cautious.

| # | Condition | Book's instruction | Page |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pregnancy | Avoid lying on your back after the first trimester; avoid abdominal pressure; ask your midwife or clinician before starting | 23 |
| 2 | Anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication; bleeding disorders | Bruising risk is higher; use substantially less pressure, and get advice first | 23 |
| 3 | Reduced sensation — peripheral neuropathy, diabetes with neuropathy, spinal cord injury | **Do not use pressure you cannot feel accurately.** The feedback loop that makes this safe is absent | 23 |
| 4 | Severe osteoporosis | Avoid loading over bone; no pressure on the spine | 23 |
| 5 | Recent surgery | No work on or near the site until cleared by the surgeon | 23 |
| 6 | Known or suspected deep vein thrombosis | **Absolute exclusion in the affected limb** | 23 |
| 7 | Peripheral arterial disease | Avoid calf pressure | 23 |
| 8 | Varicose veins | Avoid direct pressure on the veins | 23 |
| 9 | Active cancer | Get advice before any technique | 23 |
| 10 | Hypermobility spectrum disorder or hEDS | Reduce end-range work; prioritise control over range | 23 |
| 11 | Acute injury, bruising, swelling, open wound, or skin infection | Avoid the area entirely | 23 |
| 12 | Fever or acute illness | Defer until you are well | 23 |
| 13 | (See note) | | |

**Clinician questions**

6. Is item 9 (**active cancer**) too permissive? "Get advice before any technique" permits the reader to proceed on their own judgement. Should this be an absolute exclusion outside a supervised setting?
7. Is item 11 (**acute injury/swelling**) correctly absolute? Is there a case for gentle movement being *preferable* to nothing?
8. Are any of these **too restrictive** to the point of denying a well reader a safe, useful practice?
9. **What is missing?** Candidates the book does not list: recent fracture, known aneurysm, lymphoedema, pacemaker or implanted device, severe varicose veins with phlebitis history, uncontrolled hypertension, epilepsy (for the neck section), anticoagulation in pregnancy, steroid use with tissue fragility, Ehlers-Danlos vascular type.
10. **The heading tier on item 6** — "known *or suspected*" DVT. Is "suspected" defined clearly enough for a lay reader to act on?

## 1.3 The governing rule

The book's closing safety instruction is: **"When in doubt, don't."**

**Clinician question**

11. Does this rule adequately cover the reader who has *no* doubt but should have? If not, what single sentence would you add?

# Part 2 — Techniques (Chapter 5)

This is the part that most needs your attention. Each technique below is described in the book only in prose, with no illustration yet (illustrations are pending — see the separate illustration specification).

For each, please check: **position and load, tissue targeted, structures at risk, stop signals, and duration/pressure guidance.**

## The three load-control principles (pages 21–22)

Before any technique, the book sets three rules:

1. **Work at three or four out of ten** on a perceived-pressure scale.
2. **Short holds of twenty to sixty seconds**, a few times, rather than long sustained pressure.
3. **Stop** on any sharp pain, radiating pain, numbness, tingling, weakness, dizziness, or burning.

**Clinician questions**

12. Is "three or four out of ten" a usable instruction for a lay reader, given there is no validated scale behind it? Is there a better calibration?
13. Are 20–60 second holds appropriate, or should this be shorter/longer for specific regions?
14. Is the book's claim that *soreness for a few hours is normal but a flare into the next day means less pressure* clinically sound?

## 5.1 Neck-adjacent work — upper trapezius (page 28)

**Book's instruction:** ball against the wall, working the upper trapezius **at the shoulder rather than at the neck**. The book explicitly forbids self-manipulation of the neck.

**Explicitly avoided:** the front and sides of the neck (carotid arteries, airway); the back of the neck directly over the cervical spine; the base of the skull under pressure; anything reproducing dizziness, visual change, or nausea.

**Clinician questions**

15. Is "at the shoulder rather than at the neck" a clear enough boundary? Where exactly does the safe zone end?
16. **Base of the skull** — the book avoids pressure there. Is *any* contact at the suboccipital region acceptable, or should this be an absolute exclusion?
17. Is wall-based loading genuinely safer than floor-based for this region, as the book claims?
18. Should the book warn about the **carotid sinus** specifically (faintness with pressure at the anterior neck), given readers improvise?

## 5.2 Upper medial border of the scapula (page 28)

**Book's instruction:** ball against the wall, for the muscles attaching to the upper medial scapular border.

**Clinician questions**

19. Is the **dorsal scapular nerve** or the **accessory nerve** at meaningful risk here with a tennis ball against a wall?
20. Is there a risk of the reader working over the **first rib** or the **cervical-thoracic junction** without realising?

## 5.3 Thoracic spine with a rolled towel (page 28)

**Book's instruction:** rolled towel for thoracic extension, on the basis that much perceived neck stiffness originates in the upper back.

**Clinician questions**

21. Is towel-based thoracic extension safe for an unscreened general population, given the prevalence of osteoporotic vertebral fracture in older readers?
22. Should the book require a reader over a certain age, or with any fracture risk, to be assessed first?
23. Is the claim that perceived neck stiffness often originates in the upper back stated with appropriate hedging?

## 5.4 Gluteal region (page 29)

**Book's instruction:** ball under one buttock, seated or against a wall, controlling load by how much weight is taken through that side. Work the outer and upper part.

**Explicitly avoided:** sustained deep pressure where the sciatic nerve runs; any reproduction of leg pain, tingling, or numbness is a stop signal, not a target.

**Clinician questions**

24. Is the **sciatic nerve** warning sufficiently specific — does the reader know *where* not to press?
25. Is the **piriformis** region implied? If a reader presses there and reproduces sciatica, does the book's stop rule fire early enough?
26. Should **gluteal injection sites** or **ischial bursitis** be mentioned?

## 5.5 Iliotibial band (page 29)

**Book's instruction:** the ITB cannot be stretched or broken down by pressure; heavy direct work on it is uncomfortable and mechanically pointless. Work the muscle around it instead — gluteus medius at the hip, vastus lateralis on the outer thigh.

**Clinician question**

27. Is the mechanical claim (ITB not amenable to pressure) accurately stated? It is a claim the book uses to correct a common misconception, so it needs to be right.

## 5.6 Hip flexors (page 29)

**Book's instruction:** better with a towel than a ball; lie on your side with a rolled towel under the hip, or a supported half-kneeling position, letting the hip settle rather than forcing it.

**Clinician questions**

28. Is the **femoral artery/nerve** or **lymph node** region a risk in the anterior hip? The book does not warn about the anterior hip specifically.
29. Is "letting the hip settle" a meaningful instruction, or too vague to follow safely?

## 5.7 Lower back — paraspinal muscles (page 30)

**Book's instruction:** the lower back is where self-treatment most often becomes self-injury. No direct pressure on the spine or over the spinous processes. No lying on a ball on the floor with full body weight. Work the paraspinal muscle either side of the spine with the ball against a wall so the legs control the load.

**Clinician question**

30. This is the highest-risk section in the book. Is the rule set complete? Specifically: the **kidneys**, the **12th rib**, and the **costovertebral angle** are not named. Should they be?

## 5.8 Calf (page 30) — **absolute rule**

**Book's instruction:** if any DVT risk factor is present — recent surgery, prolonged immobility, long-haul travel, a clotting disorder, active cancer, pregnancy, or oestrogen therapy — **do not apply pressure to the calf**. A calf that is unilaterally swollen, warm, red, and tender needs urgent assessment, not massage.

**Clinician questions**

31. Is this list of risk factors complete? Candidates not listed: previous VTE, family history of VTE, smoking, obesity, varicose veins, age over 60, heart failure.
32. Is the wording strong enough? It is the only true absolute exclusion in the technique chapters and carries the most litigation risk.
33. Should **bilateral** calf symptoms be addressed (the book specifies unilateral)?

## 5.9 Thigh (page 30)

**Book's instruction:** quadriceps and hamstrings with a ball or roller, broad and moderate, avoiding the outer knee and the head of the fibula where the common peroneal nerve runs.

**Clinician question**

34. Is the **common peroneal nerve** warning adequately localised? Is there also a **femoral triangle** risk on the anterior thigh that is unaddressed?

## 5.10 Feet (page 30)

**Book's instruction:** "the safest and most reliably pleasant region in the book." Seated, ball under the arch, control weight through the leg. "Nothing bad happens here."

**Clinician questions**

35. Is **"Nothing bad happens here"** an acceptable absolute claim? It is the only unqualified safety claim in the book. Consider: neuropathy, Charcot foot, plantar fascia rupture, fractures in osteoporosis.
36. Should this section carry a caveat for readers with diabetes or neuropathy, given the contraindication table already excludes pressure they cannot feel?

> **THIS BOOK HAS NOT BEEN REVIEWED BY A DOCTOR, PHYSIOTHERAPIST, OR OTHER QUALIFIED CLINICIAN.**
>
> It is written for general educational purposes. It is not a substitute for individual medical assessment, diagnosis, or treatment, and no book can examine you.
>
> Read Chapter 4 before attempting anything in Chapter 5. Do not begin if you have any condition on the contraindication list, are pregnant, have recently had surgery, take anticoagulant medication, or are unsure whether these techniques are appropriate for you.
>
> The information here reflects the published evidence available in 2026. Medicine changes. If your situation changes, or if you are uncertain, consult a qualified healthcare professional.

**Clinician questions**

37. Does this notice adequately discharge a duty of care for a self-treatment book with no clinical review?
38. Is the ordering correct — should the contraindication list appear *before* the instruction to read Chapter 4?
39. Is there anything you would add, in one or two sentences, that would materially reduce risk?

## What happens with your review

1. Accepted changes are made to `book/short/04-before-you-start.md` and `book/short/05-the-routine.md`.
2. Both editions are rebuilt and re-validated.
3. Your name is credited **only if you consent** to it. If you decline, the book states that clinical review was performed without naming you.
4. The copyright-page notice changes from "has not been reviewed" to naming the review — it is not deleted, because it also covers the fact that no book can examine an individual reader.

No part of your review will be represented as an endorsement of the book's evidence claims, and no testimonial will be requested or used.
